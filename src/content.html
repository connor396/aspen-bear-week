The plan

Put the food back where it belongs.

Aspen has spent years, correctly, on locking down trash. Nobody is working on the other half of the problem: the wild food supply itself. That is the gap we exist to close.

How the work happens
An important distinction

This is habitat restoration. It is not feeding bears.

Feeding bears is illegal in Colorado, and for good reason: it concentrates animals, creates dependence, and teaches them to associate people with food. We do the opposite, and the difference is not a technicality.

Feeding

  • Food put out for animals
  • Draws bears to a single point
  • Close to people
  • Creates dependence on humans
  • Illegal, and rightly so

Restoration

  • Native plants put in the ground
  • Spreads forage across a landscape
  • Deliberately far from development
  • Restores a bear's natural range
  • Standard conservation practice

The Work

Restoring the food supply, on ground chosen with evidence.

Three stages. The first one is the one almost nobody does, and it is why this can work.

Stage 01

Find the ground.

Machine learning over satellite imagery, joined to normalised parcel records, scores candidate land on forest cover, soils, slope, aspect, water proximity and fire history, then returns who owns each parcel and how to reach them.

That matters because the mountains around Aspen are a patchwork: White River National Forest, Pitkin County Open Space, City of Aspen parcels, and private land. Each one has a different owner and a different permission path. Knowing which is which before anyone promises a location is the difference between a plan and a wish.

Scored on
Slope, aspect, soils, existing oak and aspen cover, water proximity, fire history, distance from development.
Returns
A ranked shortlist with ownership and jurisdiction identified for every parcel.
Sequencing
Private land and county open space first. Federal land needs written authorisation and takes far longer.
Stage 02

Replant native forage.

Colorado Parks and Wildlife already tracks the species that carry bears through autumn: Gambel oak, serviceberry, chokecherry, hawthorn and pinon pine. Colorado's largest black bear populations sit where Gambel oak and aspen meet open serviceberry and chokecherry. We put those species back, at scale, far from where people live.

Stage 03

Bear Aware.

Roughly half the problem is people who have never been told the rules. Visitors arrive, leave a car unlocked with food in it, put a bin out the night before, and create a habituated bear that someone else has to deal with. Education is not the soft part of this work. It is the part with the fastest return.

Bear Aware

What everyone in this valley should know.

Four things. None of them take more than two minutes, and between them they prevent most of what goes wrong.

Guide 01

Why there are so many bears this year

2026 is on track to be the worst bear year Colorado has recorded since statewide digital tracking began in 2019. Low snowpack and drought failed the berry, acorn and nut crops across much of the state.

From late summer bears enter hyperphagia and can spend up to twenty hours a day feeding to build the fat they need to survive winter. When the mountain has nothing, that search reaches town.

Source: Colorado Parks and Wildlife reporting, 2026
Guide 02

If you see a bear

  • If it has not seen you, move away quietly and give it a clear escape route.
  • If it has, stand still, talk in a normal voice and stay calm.
  • Back away slowly. Never run, which can trigger a chase.
  • Never get between a mother and her cubs.
  • Never take a selfie and never crowd it for a photo.
  • Rule of thumb: if your thumb at arm's length does not cover the animal, you are too close.
Source: Colorado Parks and Wildlife guidance
Guide 03

The trash rules, plainly

  • Wildlife-resistant containers are required, and they must be latched and secured.
  • Bins go out after 6am on collection day and back in by 7pm the same day.
  • Fines escalate: $250, then $500, then $1,000.
  • Pitkin County Code Title 6, Section 6.44 carries the county requirement.

Take down bird feeders, clean the grill, keep pet food indoors, clear fruit from trees and the ground, and lock ground-floor doors and windows.

Source: City of Aspen and Pitkin County code
Guide 04

Restoration is not feeding

Intentionally feeding or luring bears is illegal in Colorado under C.R.S. 33-6-131, and in 2026 the penalties increased: the standard changed from "intentionally" to "knowingly", the first-offence warning was removed, and the third-offence fine rose to $5,000.

Putting native plants into the ground far from development is habitat restoration, a standard conservation practice. Nothing is ever placed out for an animal to find.

Source: C.R.S. 33-6-131; Colorado HB26-1342, signed May 2026

Why this and not something else

The gap is the food, not the trash.

Aspen and Pitkin County have worked on attractants for years, and they have worked on them well: mandatory wildlife-resistant containers, escalating fines, and county grant money buying bear-resistant cans across the valley.

What nobody is funding is the wild food supply itself. That is the whole reason this organisation exists. Duplicating the trash work would add nothing.
