Bear Aware

What everyone in this valley should know.

Four things. None of them take more than two minutes, and between them they prevent most of what goes wrong.

Guide 01

Why there are so many bears this year

2026 is on track to be the worst bear year Colorado has recorded since statewide digital tracking began in 2019. Low snowpack and drought failed the berry, acorn and nut crops across much of the state.

From late summer bears enter hyperphagia and can spend up to twenty hours a day feeding to build the fat they need to survive winter. When the mountain has nothing, that search reaches town.

Source: Colorado Parks and Wildlife reporting, 2026
Guide 02

If you see a bear

  • If it has not seen you, move away quietly and give it a clear escape route.
  • If it has, stand still, talk in a normal voice and stay calm.
  • Back away slowly. Never run, which can trigger a chase.
  • Never get between a mother and her cubs.
  • Never take a selfie and never crowd it for a photo.
  • Rule of thumb: if your thumb at arm's length does not cover the animal, you are too close.
Source: Colorado Parks and Wildlife guidance
Guide 03

The trash rules, plainly

  • Wildlife-resistant containers are required, and they must be latched and secured.
  • Bins go out after 6am on collection day and back in by 7pm the same day.
  • Fines escalate: $250, then $500, then $1,000.
  • Pitkin County Code Title 6, Section 6.44 carries the county requirement.

Take down bird feeders, clean the grill, keep pet food indoors, clear fruit from trees and the ground, and lock ground-floor doors and windows.

Source: City of Aspen and Pitkin County code
Guide 04

Restoration is not feeding

Intentionally feeding or luring bears is illegal in Colorado under C.R.S. 33-6-131, and in 2026 the penalties increased: the standard changed from "intentionally" to "knowingly", the first-offence warning was removed, and the third-offence fine rose to $5,000.

Putting native plants into the ground far from development is habitat restoration, a standard conservation practice. Nothing is ever placed out for an animal to find.

The difference is where the food comes from and how it behaves. Placed food sits in one spot, arrives with a human smell, and teaches a bear that people mean calories. A restored hillside produces mast the way it did before the drought, spread across terrain, on a bear’s own schedule, with nobody present.

It also matters which hillside. This year the failure was concentrated at lower elevations, where a late spring freeze killed the blossom before fruit set; higher ground held more mast and caught more of the monsoon. Bears come down to that low and mid-elevation belt every autumn whether or not anyone plants anything. We are repairing the belt they already travel to, well away from housing. We are not building a new destination.

Source: C.R.S. 33-6-131; Colorado HB26-1342, signed May 2026; Colorado Parks and Wildlife, August 2026
Guide 05

If a bear is in the neighbourhood, scare it

A bear on a trail and a bear on your street need opposite responses. In the backcountry you give it room. In town, the kindest thing you can do is make it uncomfortable enough to leave.

  • From a safe place indoors or in a vehicle, make noise: yell, blow a whistle, bang pots, set off the car alarm or horn.
  • Do it in the time you would otherwise spend filming it. A photograph teaches a bear nothing; a fright teaches it that houses are unpleasant.
  • Never corner a bear or block its exit, and never haze a sow with cubs between you and them.
  • A bear that keeps its fear of people keeps its life. Bears remember bad experiences near homes exactly as well as they remember good ones.
Source: BearWise® and Colorado Parks and Wildlife, who partnered on the programme in 2025
Who else does this work

We are not the first people here.

The Roaring Fork Valley Bear Coalition has run bear education and a bear-resistant container programme along this corridor for years. The City of Aspen and Pitkin County write and enforce the trash rules above, and Colorado Parks and Wildlife funds conflict-reduction work across the valley. Aspen Bear Week exists to work on the wild forage, which is the part nobody has taken on. It is not a replacement for any of that, and if you are deciding where to put your time, theirs is live today.
